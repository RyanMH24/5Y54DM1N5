## What MDM actually does

You've already met [MDM](/glossary/mdm): software that enrolls, configures,
secures, and monitors [endpoints](/glossary/endpoint) — in this lesson,
specifically Apple devices like iPhones, iPads, and Macs. Rather than an
admin walking around setting up every device by hand, MDM lets them push
configuration, enforce security requirements, and take action on a device
remotely, at the scale of hundreds or thousands of machines at once.

## Enrollment: how a device joins MDM

Before MDM can manage a device, that device has to be **enrolled** — a
one-time registration step that establishes a trusted management
relationship between the device and the MDM platform. Once enrolled, the
device checks in periodically, applying whatever configuration the admin
has assigned to it and reporting its status back.

## Configuration profiles: pushing settings at scale

A **configuration profile** is a bundle of settings — Wi-Fi credentials, a
required passcode policy, which apps should be installed, restrictions on
what the device can do — that MDM pushes to every device it applies to.
Instead of configuring each device by hand, an admin builds the profile
once and assigns it to a group; every device in that group picks it up
automatically. Updating the profile updates every device that has it.

## When a device is lost: remote lock and wipe

This is where MDM earns its keep operationally. If a managed device is
reported lost or stolen, an admin can **remote lock** it (require the
passcode again, immediately, from anywhere) or, if there's real risk to
company data, **remote wipe** it entirely — erasing its contents over the
network without physical access to the device. Being able to act within
minutes of a report, rather than hoping the device is never misused, is the
whole reason enrollment is worth the upfront effort.
