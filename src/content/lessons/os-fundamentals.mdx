## What an operating system is for

An operating system (OS) sits between a computer's hardware and the
programs people actually want to run. Its job is to manage shared
resources — the CPU, memory, storage, network — and hand them out to
programs safely, so an app doesn't need to know how to talk to a disk
controller directly. Windows, macOS, and Linux are all operating systems;
every [endpoint](/glossary/endpoint) you'll support runs one.

## Processes: programs while they're running

When you open an app, the OS starts a **process** — a running instance of
that program, with its own slice of memory and its own view of what it's
currently doing. A single program can often run as multiple processes at
once (open two browser windows, get two processes). The OS is constantly
juggling many processes, giving each one a slice of CPU time so everything
feels like it's running simultaneously, even on hardware with far fewer CPU
cores than there are running processes.

This matters in support work because "the app is frozen" and "the process is
pegging the CPU" are different problems with different fixes — one is often
solved by waiting or restarting the app, the other by figuring out *why*
that process is consuming so much.

## The filesystem: where everything lives

The OS also organizes storage into a **filesystem** — a hierarchy of
directories (folders) and files. `C:\Users\jordan\Documents` on Windows and
`/home/jordan/Documents` on Linux are both paths through a filesystem
hierarchy, just with different conventions. Permissions attached to files
and directories (who can read, write, or execute them) are one of the most
common things a sysadmin configures and troubleshoots.

## Why this is foundational

Almost every support task eventually touches the OS layer: a process that
won't quit, a permission that's blocking a file, storage that's run out of
space, an update that needs installing. Knowing roughly what the OS is
doing under the hood — managing processes, organizing files, mediating
access to hardware — makes it much easier to reason about why something
broke instead of just guessing.
