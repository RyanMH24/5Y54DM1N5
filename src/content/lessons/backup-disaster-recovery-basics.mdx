## Backup types

A **full backup** copies everything, every time — simplest to restore
from, slowest and most storage-hungry to create. An **incremental backup**
copies only what changed since the last backup (of any kind) — fast and
small, but restoring means replaying the full backup plus every
incremental since it, in order. A **differential backup** copies everything
changed since the last *full* backup — a middle ground: faster to restore
than a long incremental chain, more storage than an incremental.

## The 3-2-1 rule

A widely used rule of thumb: keep **3** copies of your data, on **2**
different types of media, with **1** copy offsite. The reasoning behind
each number matters more than the rule itself — one copy is no backup at
all (a single failure destroys everything), copies on identical media can
fail the same way at the same time, and a backup stored in the same
building as the original doesn't survive a fire or flood that takes out
the building.

## RTO and RPO: the two numbers that define a recovery plan

[RTO](/glossary/rto) (Recovery Time Objective) is how long the business
can tolerate a system being down. [RPO](/glossary/rpo) (Recovery Point
Objective) is how much data loss — measured in time since the last good
backup — is acceptable. A system backed up nightly has an RPO of up to 24
hours: if it fails at 11pm, you lose a full day's work restoring last
night's backup. These two numbers, not vague goals like "back it up
regularly," are what an actual disaster recovery plan is built around.

## Why this matters day to day

"We have backups" isn't the same as "we have a recovery plan" — a backup
nobody has ever tried restoring from is an unverified assumption, not a
safety net. The questions that actually matter are: how often do we back
up (does that match our RPO), how long would a real restore take (does
that match our RTO), and when did we last actually test it.
